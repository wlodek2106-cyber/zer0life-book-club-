# Zer0Life Social Intelligence Field Guide

A practical guide to understanding people without pretending to read minds or control them.

## 1. Observe before interpreting
- Notice what a person says, does and chooses over time.
- Compare behaviour with the person's own baseline; do not treat one gesture, glance or posture as proof of lying.
- Ask open questions and check your interpretation: "Did I understand you correctly?"
- Consider context: culture, stress, fatigue, neurodiversity and the situation can change nonverbal behaviour.

## 2. Listen for meaning
Use three layers:
1. Content: what was said?
2. Emotion: what might the person be feeling? Ask rather than assume.
3. Need: what matters to them in this situation?

Try: "What is most important to you here?" and "What would a good outcome look like?"

## 3. Understand relationship patterns
- Attachment styles are useful concepts for reflection, not fixed labels or diagnoses.
- Healthy relationships allow boundaries, disagreement, privacy and independent friendships.
- Look for repeated patterns, repair after conflict and consistency between words and actions.
- Do not diagnose a partner, colleague or family member from a checklist.

## 4. Recognise manipulation and coercive control
Warning patterns can include:
- Gaslighting: persistent attempts to make someone doubt their memory or perception.
- Emotional blackmail: threats, guilt or fear used to force compliance.
- Isolation: discouraging access to friends, family, work or support.
- Moving goalposts: changing conditions so the other person can never succeed.
- Love-bombing followed by punishment, withdrawal or control.
- Monitoring, intimidation, financial restriction or threats.

One incident may have several explanations; assess patterns, impact and safety. If you feel unsafe, seek trusted support and professional local services.

## 5. Influence ethically
- Be transparent about your goal and interests.
- Give accurate information and disclose relevant trade-offs.
- Ask permission before advising or persuading.
- Respect "no" without punishment.
- Seek agreements where both parties retain meaningful choice.

Useful tools: active listening, principled negotiation, clear requests, evidence, empathy and fair boundaries.

## 6. The "puppet" test: am I being controlled?
Pause and ask:
- Can I disagree without fear of retaliation?
- Am I free to take time and consult someone I trust?
- Are the rules clear and consistent?
- Am I being pressured to hide things, cut off support or act against my values?
- Can I say no and keep my dignity, resources and safety?

If several answers are no, slow down, document concerning events where safe, reconnect with trusted people and consider professional support.

## 7. Practice exercises
### Exercise A — Observation vs. story
Write three columns: observable fact, my interpretation, other plausible explanations. Do not turn interpretation into fact.

### Exercise B — Listening
In a 10-minute conversation, summarise the other person's point before offering your own. Ask whether your summary is accurate.

### Exercise C — Boundary
Prepare one respectful sentence: "I am not comfortable with that. I can do X, but I cannot agree to Y."

### Exercise D — Persuasion audit
Before making a pitch, write down what the other person gains, what they risk, what you gain and what information they need to decide freely.

## 8. Editorial standards for the library
- Distinguish peer-reviewed research, established clinical guidance, sociology, historical texts and popular self-help.
- Do not claim that body language reliably detects lies or reveals hidden thoughts.
- Do not treat personality colour systems as validated clinical tests.
- Never use these materials to diagnose another person.
- Full books and translations remain metadata-only until commercial distribution rights for the exact edition are verified.

**Goal:** become more perceptive, empathetic, assertive and resistant to coercion—not more controlling.
